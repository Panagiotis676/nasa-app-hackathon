Hear how our planet changes

Earth Jukebox turns NASA Earth maps into sound. Close your eyes and hear where the lights of our cities are, how bright they are, and how they grew from 2012 to 2023. Everything works with a keyboard and a screen reader.

  1. Press “Start listening” (or Space). The sound sweeps across the map: west in your left ear, east in your right.
  2. Listen: higher notes are further north, louder means more light.
  3. Then try C to hear only what changed, E to explore the map, or add your own NASA maps.

Bring your own data

Your own maps: files and settings

Your maps play in the player above. Here you add the images and tell the app how to read their colours. Your images never leave your browser.

  1. Save map images from the NASA Earth Information Center, one per date, all with the same view. Name them so they sort in time order, for example 2015.png, 2020.png.
  2. Add them with “Add your own maps” in the player, or with the file picker below.
  3. Under “Colours and value range”, choose the map’s colour scale (or upload a picture of its colorbar) and type the lowest and highest values printed on it.

    The idea

    How it works

    1. 01

      Colour to number

      Every pixel is matched back to the data value it stands for, using the map’s colour scale or a picture of its colorbar. For maps without one, like night lights, brightness is the value.

    2. 02

      A sweep across the planet

      The sound travels from west, in your left ear, to east, in your right ear. The map is cut into 12 bands of latitude on a pentatonic scale: north is high, south is low, and a higher value is louder. Silence means nothing there.

    3. 03

      Change over time

      Years play in order on the same loudness scale, so change stays audible. A rising chime between frames means the average went up and a falling one means it went down.

    4. 04

      Hear only the change

      “Only what changed” (or the C key) plays the last frame minus the first in one sweep: a bright, clean tone where it went up, a low buzz where it went down. The voice then names the places that changed the most.

    Colours decoded from an image are an approximation of the true data.

    No mouse needed

    Keyboard shortcuts

    • Space play or pause
    • Right arrow or N next frame
    • Left arrow or P previous frame
    • R replay the frame
    • D describe the frame
    • L how to listen
    • C switch between every frame and only what changed
    • + or - faster or slower
    • E explore the map: the arrow keys move around it (Shift for bigger steps), or click / tap the map
    • Enter in Explore, say the place and the value
    • Esc or E leave Explore
    • T take the 1-minute tour (any key stops it)
    • ? read all the shortcuts

    Earth Jukebox

    Made for the NASA Space Apps Challenge 2026, “The Earth Information Jukebox”, by Chrysanthos Spyrou, Panayiotis Agathangelou and Chara Charalambous.

    Data

    Night lights: NASA Black Marble, NASA Scientific Visualization Studio. Air pollution example: NASA satellite images of nitrogen dioxide (NO₂) over the north-eastern United States, March 2015–19 average and March 2020. Additional NASA maps: forest height, aerosols, ocean temperature, night-light change, lightning, carbon dioxide, and sea surface temperature.
